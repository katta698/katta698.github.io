AWS Architecture Series #89 — Your app picks the role; the trust policy decides whether it may

An identity pool has two authentication flows that end in the same place and decide the role in different places. In the enhanced flow the pool picks the role. In the basic flow your application does — and AWS states the boundary plainly: the app can request credentials for any IAM role 'that you have configured with a sufficient trust policy'.

Oct 21, 2026 14 min read
✓Verified against current vendor documentation on 21 October 2026. Pricing, limits and API behaviour were checked against the official docs on that date. Cloud services change fast — if you are reading this much later, treat the specifics as a starting point and re-check the linked sources.

Business Challenge

An identity pool is the thing that turns a login into AWS credentials. “Amazon Cognito… delivers temporary, limited-privilege credentials to your application to access AWS resources.”

“Amazon Cognito has two different flows for authentication with public providers: enhanced and basic.” They reach the same destination, and the difference between them is one sentence long.

Enhanced: “Enhanced authentication manages the logic of IAM role selection and credentials retrieval in your identity pool configuration.”

Basic: “When you implement the basic authentication flow, your application selects the IAM role that you want users to assume.”

1In the basic flow the client picks the role, and the trust policy is what decides whether it may

AWS states the scope of that plainly, and it is broader than "the role you configured":

“The AssumeRoleWithWebIdentity request in the classic workflow grants your app a greater ability to request credentials for any AWS Identity and Access Management role that you have configured with a sufficient trust policy.”

Read that as an inventory question rather than a feature description. The set of roles your client can obtain is not the set you intended it to use — it is the set whose trust policies happen to accept a token from this identity pool. Any role in the account with a loose enough cognito-identity.amazonaws.com trust relationship is reachable, whether or not anyone thought of it as a mobile app role.

And the documentation is unusually direct about what that depends on: “To implement basic authentication, first evaluate the trust relationships of your IAM roles for web identities. Then build the logic for role selection into your client and secure the client against modification by users.”

"Secure the client against modification by users" is a remarkable sentence to find in a security guide, and it is the honest one. Role selection in the client is a decision made on a device you do not control.

Fix

Prefer the enhanced flow, which AWS calls “the most secure choice with the lowest level of developer effort”. If you are on the basic flow, the audit is not of your app — it is of every role in the account whose trust policy names cognito-identity.amazonaws.com. That list is your actual blast radius, and nothing in the identity pool shortens it.

2You cannot have role mappings and the basic flow, and AWS blocks the combination

This is the detail that makes the design coherent, and it is easy to miss because it appears as an error string:

“When you attempt GetOpenIdToken in an identity pool with role mappings, you receive the following error. Basic (classic) flow is not supported with RoleMappings, please use enhanced flow.”

And the corollary, stated as a property of pools that do support the basic flow: “You can sign in with the Basic authflow in user pools that don't have role mappings. This type of identity pool doesn't have a default authenticated or unauthenticated role, and doesn't have role-based or attribute-based access control configured.”

So the two models are mutually exclusive by enforcement rather than by convention. You cannot configure careful pool-side role mapping and then let a client select its own role — which matters because that configuration would look like a control while controlling nothing.

Read that sentence for what it says, though: AWS is describing this type of identity pool — one configured for basic-flow use. It is not a rule that a pool lacking rule-based mappings therefore has no default roles. Default authenticated and unauthenticated roles and rule-based role mappings are separate pieces of configuration, and the common case is a pool with the former and none of the latter. What matters for the basic flow is that neither of them bounds the client's choice.

This is the service refusing to let you hold a false belief. The alternative design, where both are configured and the client silently wins, is the one that produces an audit finding two years later.

Fix

Treat the choice as architectural and made once. If your pool has role mappings, the basic flow is not available to you and your role-selection logic belongs in the pool. And keep two things separate: an identity pool can have default authenticated and unauthenticated IAM roles even without rule-based role mappings. In the basic authentication flow, however, the application explicitly selects the role for the AssumeRoleWithWebIdentity request. Those pool defaults do not constrain which roles the client can request; the selected role's trust policy determines whether the request succeeds.

3A guest identity is still an identity, and it still has a role

Guest access is a first-class configuration choice, not an absence of one: “In Configure identity pool trust, choose to set up your identity pool for Authenticated access, Guest access, or both”, and then “In Configure permissions, choose a default IAM role for authenticated or guest users in your identity pool.”

So an unauthenticated caller does not get no credentials. It gets credentials from a role: “You can also define a separate IAM role with limited permissions for guest users who are not authenticated.”

What separates the two at the IAM layer is one condition in the trust policy. AWS's example restricts the aud to the pool, and then: “the policy specifies that one of the array members of the multi-value amr claim of the token issued by the Amazon Cognito GetOpenIdToken API action has the value authenticated.”

Which means the authenticated role is protected from guest tokens by the presence of that amr condition and by nothing else. Omit it and a guest token satisfies a trust policy written for signed-in users.

Fix

Read the trust policy of your authenticated role and confirm the amr condition is there. AWS is explicit about the purpose: the trust policy exists so the role “can only be assumed by Amazon Cognito for authenticated users in your identity pool” — and both halves of that sentence, the pool restriction and the authenticated restriction, are separate conditions you have to have written.

Architecture

A token in, an identity ID in the middle, AWS credentials out — and a decision about where the role is chosen that changes the whole security model.

What the pool accepts, and the one asymmetry in it

ProviderAuthentication artifact
Amazon Cognito user poolID token
OpenID Connect (OIDC)ID token
SAML 2.0SAML assertion
Social providerAccess token

Three of those are assertions about identity. The fourth is a bearer credential for an API. That is not a defect — it is what social providers issue — but it is worth knowing that the artifact your pool trusts from a social login is a different kind of object from the one it trusts from OIDC.

The two flows, step for step

Enhanced (simplified)Basic (classic)
Order of operations GetId → GetCredentialsForIdentity GetId → GetOpenIdToken → AssumeRoleWithWebIdentity
Who selects the role The pool — it “manages the logic of IAM role selection” Your application
What bounds the choice Pool configuration: default role, RBAC or ABAC “any… role that you have configured with a sufficient trust policy”
Credential lifetime “valid for one hour” “You can also request a custom role session duration”
Calls AWS STS directly No — “doesn't need to make additional API requests to AWS STS” Yes
Works with role mappings Yes No — the request errors
The token in the middle of the basic flow is issued by your pool, not by your provider

“GetOpenIdToken returns a new OAuth 2.0 token that is issued by your identity pool.”

That is the token STS evaluates, and it is why the trust policy conditions are written against cognito-identity.amazonaws.com and the pool's own aud and amr claims rather than against your upstream provider. The provider's assertion is consumed earlier and does not reach STS.

Where role selection can be configured, when the pool is doing it

“Permissions for each user are controlled through IAM roles that you create. You can define rules to choose the role for each user based on claims in the user's ID token.” And the enhanced flow's own mechanism: “The GetCredentialsForIdentity request of the enhanced authflow requests a role based on the contents of an access token.”

So in the enhanced flow the claims decide, evaluated server-side by the pool. In the basic flow the client decides and the claims only matter insofar as a trust policy condition tests them. Same claims, two completely different enforcement points.

And one permission that lets someone exceed their own access

“To allow a user to set roles with permissions in excess of the user's existing permissions on an identity pool, grant them iam:PassRole permission to pass the role to the set-identity-pool-roles API.”

Whoever can set the pool's roles can grant the pool's users more than that person holds. That is how PassRole is supposed to work, and it means the right to configure an identity pool is a privilege-escalation path unless the roles that can be passed are constrained.

One configuration choice that cannot be undone

“If you configure a Custom developer provider, you can't modify or delete it after you create your identity pool.”

Worth noting at creation time rather than discovering later, because the developer-authenticated flow is the one where the pool trusts you completely: “the claims that you present in your request for an identity are arbitrary identifiers, and the authentication is authorized by IAM credentials that you encode in your application.”

Why This Architecture Holds Up

Refusing to combine the two models is the best decision here

The error string is the whole argument: “Basic (classic) flow is not supported with RoleMappings, please use enhanced flow.”

A service that allowed both would let you configure role mapping, read it back in the console, show it to an auditor, and have it decide nothing because the client passes its own role ARN to STS. Making the combination an error means the configuration you can see is the configuration that applies. That is worth more than the flexibility it removes.

The enhanced flow is recommended on grounds that are actually about security

“The enhanced flow is the most secure choice with the lowest level of developer effort”, because “Your identity pool evaluates your users for the IAM role credentials that they should receive. You don't need to embed logic for role selection in your client.”

Moving a decision off an untrusted device and into a service is the actual security property, and AWS names it rather than selling the flow on request count. The request-count benefit is real and listed second.

Telling you to secure the client is better than implying you need not

“build the logic for role selection into your client and secure the client against modification by users” is an uncomfortable instruction, and it is the correct one. The basic flow's security depends on client integrity; saying so lets a reader decide whether that assumption holds for a mobile app or a single-page application. The alternative — documenting the flow as "more granular control" and stopping — would leave the dependency unstated.

It is paired with an explicit default: “When you create a new identity pool, don't activate basic (classic) authentication by default, as a best practice.”

The audit worth running

List every IAM role in the account whose trust policy has Federated: cognito-identity.amazonaws.com. For each one, check two conditions: is the aud pinned to a specific pool, and is there an amr condition. A role missing the first is reachable from any of your pools; a role missing the second is reachable by guests.

Then establish which flow each client uses. If any client uses the basic flow, the list above is the set of roles it can request — not the one role you had in mind. That is the number to put in front of whoever owns the risk.

Closing Thought

The interesting thing about identity pools is not that the basic flow is riskier — it is that AWS made the two models impossible to mix and said so with an error rather than a warning. You cannot have a role-mapping configuration that looks authoritative while a client chooses for itself. One or the other.

That decision is worth noticing because the series keeps finding the opposite shape. #84 had a filter that looked like a boundary and was not. #88 had a lifecycle attached to a handle rather than to the secret. Here, the one configuration that could have misled you is the one the service refuses to let you create.

What remains your job is the sentence about trust policies. In the basic flow the bound on what your app can request is not in the pool at all — it is distributed across every role in the account that happens to trust Cognito. That list is discoverable, and it is the only version of "what can this app get" that is true.
