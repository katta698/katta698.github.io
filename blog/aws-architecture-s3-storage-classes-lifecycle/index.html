AWS Architecture Series #14 — S3 Storage Classes: Why “Just Move It to Glacier” Made the Bill Go Up

Every S3 cost mechanism is priced per object. Minimum billable sizes, minimum durations and per-object metadata decide the bill — which is why archiving billions of small files can cost more than leaving them alone.

Aug 7, 2026 13 min read

Business Challenge

A telemetry platform writes device events to S3. Four billion objects, averaging 20 KB each — roughly 80 TB. Finance asked for a storage reduction, and the team did the obvious thing: a lifecycle policy moving objects to Standard-IA after 30 days, then to Glacier Deep Archive after 90.

The next invoice was higher. Not marginally — noticeably. Nobody had done anything wrong in the sense of misconfiguring a rule. The rule was exactly what the documentation shows. The problem was that every cost mechanism in S3 is priced per object, and this bucket had four billion of them.

1A 20 KB object is billed as 128 KB in the IA classes

S3 Standard-IA, One Zone-IA and Glacier Instant Retrieval all carry a 128 KB minimum billable object size. Below that, you pay for 128 KB regardless. At an average of 20 KB, the platform was billed for 6.4× the bytes it actually stored the moment objects transitioned. The per-GB rate is lower in IA; it is not 6.4× lower.

Fix

Compare classes on billed bytes, not stored bytes. Below roughly 128 KB, S3 Standard is often cheaper than any IA class.

2Deep Archive adds 40 KB of metadata to every object

Glacier Flexible Retrieval and Deep Archive require 40 KB of additional metadata per archived object — 32 KB charged at the Glacier rate and 8 KB charged at the S3 Standard rate. For a 20 KB object that is three times the object's own size, and part of it is billed at the most expensive rate on the list. Four billion objects means 160 TB of metadata sitting under 80 TB of data.

Fix

Deep Archive is priced for large objects. Small ones should be aggregated before they get there, or not sent at all.

3The second transition triggered an early-deletion charge

Standard-IA has a 30-day minimum storage duration, and AWS is explicit that objects transitioned to a different storage class before that point are charged the same as objects deleted early. The policy moved objects to IA at day 30 and onward at day 90, which is fine — but an earlier iteration used day 45, and every object paid a pro-rated charge for IA storage it never used.

Fix

Every hop in a lifecycle chain must respect the minimum duration of the class it is leaving, not just the one it is entering.

4Intelligent-Tiering would not have saved them either

The instinct after all this is to hand the problem to S3 Intelligent-Tiering and stop thinking about it. But objects smaller than 128 KB are never monitored and never tier — they stay in the Frequent Access tier permanently. For this bucket, Intelligent-Tiering would have moved nothing at all.

Fix

Intelligent-Tiering solves unpredictable access. It does not solve small objects, because nothing does except making them bigger.

Architecture

The storage class table is usually read as a ladder from expensive-and-fast to cheap-and-slow. That reading is what produces bills like the one above, because it hides the two columns that actually decide the cost.

What each class actually asks of you

S3 Standard

No minimum duration, no minimum billable size, no retrieval fee. The only class with no strings, which is why it wins more often than its headline rate suggests.

Standard-IA & One Zone-IA

30-day minimum, 128 KB minimum billable, per-GB retrieval fee. Designed for large, long-lived objects read about once a month.

Glacier Instant Retrieval

90-day minimum, 128 KB minimum billable, retrieval fee — but millisecond access. The archive class that does not feel like an archive.

Flexible Retrieval & Deep Archive

90 and 180-day minimums, 40 KB metadata per object, and you must call RestoreObject before reading anything. Minutes to hours, or hours.

The order to make the decisions in

1

How big are the objects?

Under 128 KB, most of the cheaper classes bill you as if they were 128 KB. This question comes first because a "no" here rules out most of the table.

2

How long will they live?

Shorter than the minimum duration and you pay it anyway. Data with a 60-day life has no business in Deep Archive's 180-day minimum.

3

Do you know the access pattern?

If yes, pick a class directly. If genuinely not — and the objects are over 128 KB — that is what Intelligent-Tiering is for, and it charges no retrieval fees.

4

How fast do you need it back?

Only now does retrieval time matter. Instant Retrieval for milliseconds, Flexible for minutes to hours, Deep Archive for hours.

Intelligent-Tiering, precisely

Three automatic tiers, all with millisecond access: objects start in Frequent Access, move to Infrequent Access after 30 consecutive days without access, and to Archive Instant Access after 90. Two further tiers are optional and asynchronous — Archive Access after 90 days, Deep Archive Access after 180 — and objects in those must be restored before they can be read.

There are no retrieval fees at all. You pay a per-object monitoring and automation charge instead, which is the trade: predictable small cost, no penalty for guessing access wrong.

Why This Architecture Holds Up

Object count is the hidden variable

Storage conversations happen in terabytes, but almost every S3 cost lever is priced per object: the 128 KB minimum, the 40 KB metadata, the Intelligent-Tiering monitoring charge, the lifecycle transition request itself. A petabyte in 500 MB files behaves exactly as the pricing page suggests. The same petabyte in 20 KB files behaves nothing like it.

The most effective change available to the platform in this scenario was not a storage class at all. It was aggregation — rolling those 20 KB events into hourly or daily objects before they ever reach a lifecycle rule. Fewer, larger objects make every subsequent decision cheaper, and they make Deep Archive behave the way people expect it to.

The number to look up first

Average object size in the bucket, not total size. S3 Storage Lens reports it. If it is under 128 KB, the tiering conversation should pause until the aggregation conversation has happened.

"Access" means something specific

Intelligent-Tiering's tiering clock is reset by access — but only by certain calls. GetObject, PutObject, RestoreObject, CopyObject and CompleteMultipartUpload count. HeadObject, ListObjects, GetObjectTagging and PutObjectTagging do not.

Which matters more than it sounds: an inventory job that lists a bucket nightly, or a compliance scanner reading tags, does not keep objects warm. Meanwhile a replication job or a batch copy does tier objects back up to Frequent Access, and can quietly undo months of tiering across a whole bucket.

The asymmetry worth knowing

SelectObjectContent prevents an object tiering down to the archive tiers, but does not tier it back up to Frequent Access. It is the one call that behaves differently in each direction.

Lifecycle rules are cheap to write and expensive to get wrong

A transition is billed as a request, per object, and moving four billion objects is a real one-off cost before any saving accrues. Worse, if the destination class turns out to be the wrong choice, moving them back costs again — plus early-deletion charges if the minimum duration has not elapsed.

This asymmetry argues for restraint. It is cheap to leave data in S3 Standard while you measure, and expensive to shuffle a large bucket twice because the first policy was written from intuition.

Key Architecture Decisions

Decision Choice Reasoning
Objects under 128 KB Aggregate, or leave in S3 Standard IA and Glacier Instant Retrieval bill a 128 KB minimum, and Intelligent-Tiering will not tier them at all.
Unpredictable access, large objects Intelligent-Tiering No retrieval fees and no minimum duration; you pay a per-object monitoring charge instead of paying for a wrong guess.
Known cold data, needs millisecond access Glacier Instant Retrieval Archive pricing without a restore step. The 90-day minimum is the price of that convenience.
Genuine archive, large objects Deep Archive Cheapest per GB by a wide margin, but only once 40 KB of per-object metadata is a rounding error rather than a multiplier.
Lifecycle transition timing Respect the minimum of the class being left Transitioning early is charged exactly like deleting early, so a fast chain pays for storage it never used.
Recreatable data One Zone-IA Cheaper than Standard-IA because it lives in a single AZ. Appropriate for replicas and derived data, not for a primary copy.

The audit that pays for itself

Before writing any lifecycle rule, get three numbers per bucket: average object size, object count, and how much of the data has been read in the last 90 days. S3 Storage Lens gives you the first two directly. Most estates discover at least one bucket where the object count is enormous and the average size is tiny — and that bucket is the one where a well-intentioned lifecycle policy will do the most damage.

Test on a prefix first

Lifecycle rules can be scoped to a prefix. Apply the policy to a representative slice, wait for a full billing period, and compare. It costs one month of patience and saves the bill from a four-billion-object mistake.

Closing Thought

"Move it to Glacier" is repeated so often that it has stopped sounding like a decision. It is one, and the deciding input is not how old the data is or how rarely anyone reads it. It is how many objects there are and how big each one is.

What makes this consistently surprising is that the pricing page is organised by class, so it invites comparison down the per-GB column — where Deep Archive obviously wins. The minimum billable size and per-object metadata sit in footnotes and a comparison table further down the page. They are not hidden, exactly. They are just not where anyone looks when the task is "reduce storage cost".

The honest caveat: none of this makes the cheaper classes a trap. For large objects with genuinely cold access, Deep Archive is dramatically cheaper and the minimums are irrelevant. The failure mode is not choosing archive storage — it is choosing it by age alone, on a bucket whose shape makes every per-object charge land four billion times.
