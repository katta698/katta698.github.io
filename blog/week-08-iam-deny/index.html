Taking back a permission I had to give away

Last week the read-only half of my CI pipeline had to be given a role that can delete things, because Google offers no smaller one. This week it gets a custom role with exactly two permissions and a deny policy behind it — and the tool everyone reaches for to check deny policies turns out to be unable to see them.

Why — The Problem This Solves

This lab's CI runs as two separate identities. tf-plan is used during terraform plan and is supposed to be able to read and nothing else; tf-apply is used during terraform apply and can change things. The split is enforced by Google at token exchange, not by convention.

Week 7 broke it. Reading a Cloud Asset Inventory feed needs cloudasset.feeds.get, and no predefined role carries that permission without also carrying create, update and delete. The only role that works is roles/cloudasset.owner. So the read-only identity was given it, the hole was written down, and the week shipped.

A read-only identity that can delete things is not read-only. This week closes it.

What You Need to Know — Skills & Tools

Google Cloud

  • Custom roles — your own list of permissions, when no predefined role fits
  • Deny policies — refuse a permission regardless of any role
  • Policy Troubleshooter — ask what a principal can actually do
  • Principals — the IAM word for any identity: person, group or service account

Tooling

  • Terraform — google_organization_iam_custom_role, google_iam_deny_policy
  • HCP Terraform — remote runs, Workload Identity Federation
  • curl and an access token — the Troubleshooter v3 API, which gcloud does not call

Concepts to understand before starting

Deny beats allow A role is all-or-nothing Fix at source, then guard Never ship a deny without a break-glass

Architecture — How It Fits Together

Only the first box actually fires today — after the custom role swap the permission is not granted at all, so the request never reaches the deny. The second box exists for the day someone re-grants a broad role. Which is also why it had to be tested separately.

How We Built It — Step by Step

1. Measure the problem before touching anything

Policy Troubleshooter answers "can this principal do this thing?" without needing to impersonate anyone — which matters here, because tf-plan can only be assumed by HCP Terraform, never by a person.

principal:  tf-plan@…
permission: cloudasset.feeds.delete
access:     GRANTED

This step is the one people skip, and skipping it is what turns a demonstration into a claim. Without it, a refusal at the end could be a typo, a wrong permission name, anything.

2. The fix — a role that carries only what a plan reads

resource "google_organization_iam_custom_role" "plan_asset_reader" {
  org_id  = var.org_id
  role_id = "tfPlanAssetReader"
  title   = "Terraform Plan - Asset Reader"

  permissions = [
    "cloudasset.feeds.get",
    "cloudasset.feeds.list",
  ]
}

Two permissions. Everything else in roles/cloudasset.owner was a side-effect of there being no smaller predefined role.

The honest trade-off: a custom role does not track Google. A predefined role picks up new permissions automatically as a service evolves; this one will fail a plan until someone adds them by hand. That maintenance is the real price of least privilege, and it is why predefined roles still exist.

3. The guardrail — a deny policy with two exceptions

rules {
  deny_rule {
    denied_principals  = ["principalSet://goog/public:all"]
    denied_permissions = ["cloudasset.googleapis.com/feeds.delete"]

    exception_principals = [
      "principal://iam.googleapis.com/projects/-/serviceAccounts/${var.apply_service_account}",
      "principal://goog/subject/${var.break_glass_principal}",
    ]
  }
}

Scoped wider than the identity we just fixed, on purpose: denying only tf-plan would protect against the problem already solved and nothing else. The useful guardrail is "nobody deletes an asset feed by hand".

The break-glass exception is not optional. A deny with no way out is an outage waiting for the day you genuinely need the denied action, and the usual fix under pressure is to delete the entire policy — which removes the control completely instead of using it once. One named human is safer than a guardrail people learn to switch off.

Note the principal format. It is not the serviceAccount: string Terraform uses everywhere else, and using that spelling fails with "invalid principal ... as part of the exempted principals list".

4. Swap the binding, in the layer CI does not control

The custom role is created by tf-apply, but the binding that puts it on tf-plan is not: an identity must not manage its own grants. It lives in the hand-applied Week 2 configuration, same as every other CI grant in this lab.

5. Test the fix

tf-plan  / feeds.delete   ->  CANNOT_ACCESS     (was GRANTED)
tf-plan  / feeds.get      ->  CAN_ACCESS        (still works)
tf-apply / feeds.delete   ->  CAN_ACCESS        (exempted)

Three calls, because "we removed a permission" is only half an answer. The second line is what proves we did not simply break the plan identity.

6. Test the guardrail, which the fix was hiding

CANNOT_ACCESS above is the custom role doing the work. The permission is not granted at all, so the request never reaches the deny policy — meaning the guardrail was completely untested.

So simulate the future mistake. Re-grant roles/cloudasset.owner to tf-plan and ask again:

allow policies say:  ALLOW_ACCESS_STATE_GRANTED
deny policies say:   DENY_ACCESS_STATE_DENIED
overall:             CANNOT_ACCESS
matched policy:      deny-asset-feed-deletion

Deny beats allow, and the policy names itself. The over-grant was removed immediately afterwards.

Challenges — What Actually Went Wrong

1. The tool for checking deny policies cannot see deny policies

With the deny actively blocking, Policy Troubleshooter v1 answered GRANTED and v3 answered CANNOT_ACCESS. Same question, same minute. v1 evaluates allow policies only.

gcloud policy-troubleshoot iam calls v1. Had I trusted it I would have concluded the guardrail was broken and gone looking for a fault that did not exist. Name your instrument and know what it cannot see.

2. Creating a custom role requires permission to read one

The failure was not "cannot create" but "Unable to verify whether custom org role ... already exists and must be undeleted". Deleted custom roles are recoverable for seven days, so the provider reads before it writes and a create needs the get.

3. "Access denied" that was not a denial

The first after-state probes returned UNKNOWN_INFO_DENIED, which reads like a refusal and is not one — it means the caller cannot read the policies the troubleshooter would have to explain. It cleared once my own account held the two read-only roles. Second week running that running the validator needed its own grants.

Security — Controls at Every Layer

Fix at source, then guard. A deny policy on top of an unnecessary role is a compensating control for a problem you could simply remove. The custom role is the fix; the deny exists for the day someone undoes it.

The guardrail is deliberately wider than the bug. It denies everyone, not just the identity that had the problem, because the next over-grant will not be the same one.

Break-glass is part of the control, not an exception to it. Policies without a documented way out get deleted in an incident, and deleting one removes it for everybody, permanently.

The rollback was written before the apply. Deny policies beat every role including Organization Admin, so "how do I undo this" is not a question to start answering afterwards.

Cost

$0. Custom roles, deny policies and Policy Troubleshooter are free. No project was created; nothing here runs.

The real cost is maintenance. A custom role is a promise to keep it current as Google adds permissions, and nothing reminds you — it surfaces as a plan failing months later. Budget for that before choosing a custom role over a predefined one.

Cleanup

This week is exempt from teardown: removing the controls restores the hole Week 7 documented.

If you do need to lift the deny, use the break-glass exception rather than deleting the policy. Deleting it removes the control for every principal and nothing will tell you it is gone — the next over-grant simply works.

If you remove the custom role, put roles/cloudasset.owner back first, or the next terraform plan touching an asset feed fails on a permission it no longer has.

References

  • Deny policies — the v2 API, 500 per resource, and that conditions may use tag functions only.
  • Deny access to resources — the principal identifier formats, which are this week's sharpest edge.
  • IAM policy types — how allow, deny and principal access boundary policies are evaluated together.

Not answered anywhere I looked, and measured instead: that Policy Troubleshooter v1 cannot see deny policies; that creating a custom role needs permission to read one; and that UNKNOWN_INFO_DENIED describes the caller, not the principal being asked about.

Key Takeaways

  • Measure before you build. A refusal at the end proves nothing unless you showed the same command succeeding first.
  • A guardrail you have not triggered is a guess. The fix hid the deny policy completely; testing it meant deliberately recreating the mistake it exists for.
  • Know what your instrument cannot see. The default tool reported the opposite of the truth, confidently.
  • Never ship a deny without a break-glass. The alternative is someone deleting the whole policy at 2am.
