What happens to an event nobody wanted

Publish an event no rule matches and EventBridge returns HTTP 200 with an event id. Your code carries on. Nothing consumed it, nothing alarmed, and nothing recorded that a decision was made to drop it. This week builds a bus that can answer four questions about itself — and finds that each answer is narrower than it looks.

Lab Overview

Picture a noticeboard. Anyone can pin a note to it. Separately, people leave standing instructions with the office manager — copy me on anything about orders. The person pinning the note never learns who read it, and that is the point.

The problem. Now someone pins a note reading order.crated. A transposed letter. No instruction matches it — and the board still accepts it and says "got it". Nobody ever reads that note, and nothing anywhere reports a problem, because the office only counts how many notes matched each instruction. Nobody counts the notes that matched nothing.

The objective. Build an event bus you can actually interrogate: who published, what was delivered, what matched nothing, and what happens to an event nobody wanted.

The outcome. PutEvents returning 200 means the bus accepted your event and nothing more. Three separate mechanisms were needed to see past that, and each one has a hole worth knowing about.

Cloud Services and Concepts

ServicePurpose hereWhat it is
Amazon EventBridgeThe bus itself — a custom bus, not default. Accepts published events and routes them by rule.An event bus. Services and your own code publish events to it, and rules decide which events reach which targets.
EventBridge archive and replayKeeps what was published (not what was delivered) and can re-publish it later.An archive keeps a copy of what was PUBLISHED to a bus, not what was delivered, and replay pushes those stored events through the rules again.
Schema discoveryInfers event shapes from real traffic and writes them into a registry.Watches real traffic on a bus and infers the shape of the events flowing through it, recording what producers actually send rather than what they were supposed to send.
AWS CloudTrailData events record who published, not what was delivered.Records who called which AWS API, when, and from where. It answers who did it, not what the data contained.
AWS Lambda and Amazon SQSThe two subscribers, plus the queues that catch two different kinds of failure.Lambda runs the code; SQS is a queue that holds messages until something collects them, including the ones that failed to deliver.
Amazon CloudWatchPer-rule MatchedEvents, and the alarm on consumer errors.AWS's metrics and logs service. Most services publish numbers into it automatically, without being asked to.

A 200 from PutEvents is an acceptance receipt, not a delivery receipt. It does not mean anything was listening, or that anything was delivered.

There is no metric for "matched no rule". You get MatchedEvents per rule and nothing for the gap between them. That gap has to be constructed — and the only way to construct it costs a second delivery of every event.

Architecture and Workflow

  1. A publisher calls PutEvents against a custom bus and receives 200 plus an event id.
  2. The orders rule matches on source and detail-type; matching events go to a Lambda consumer.
  3. A catch-all rule matches everything and writes to a log group, so catch-all minus orders yields the count of events that matched nothing.
  4. Delivery failures go to a rule-target DLQ; a consumer that accepts an event and then fails needs its own on-failure destination — two failure modes, two queues.
  5. An archive retains what was published, independently of whether anything consumed it.
  6. CloudTrail data events record who called PutEvents, scoped by ARN to this one bus.
  7. Schema discovery infers shapes from the real traffic, including the mistakes.

Design decisions

A custom bus, not default. Scoping CloudTrail data events to one bus by ARN is only possible with a bus of your own — and an unscoped selector logs every PutEvents in the account and bills for all of it.

A catch-all rule purely to make a metric exist. This is the only way to detect unmatched events, and you pay for it with a second delivery of every event.

Two dead-letter paths, deliberately. They catch different failures, which most designs discover the hard way.

Implementation

One custom bus, two subscribers and three watching mechanisms. The lab guide carries the full Terraform and the publish/replay scripts.

The unmatched-event detector is arithmetic, not a feature:

catch-all MatchedEvents  -  orders MatchedEvents  =  matched nothing

There is no metric for it, so the catch-all rule exists purely to make one half of that subtraction exist.

Security and Cost

Security

No static AWS credentials. HCP Terraform authenticates by OIDC.

The trail is scoped by ARN to one bus. An unscoped data-event selector logs every PutEvents in the account — including other projects' — and bills for all of it.

The DLQ's resource policy names the specific rule. Being a dead-letter queue grants a queue nothing; without the aws:SourceArn condition, any EventBridge rule in any account could write to it.

Log retention set explicitly. An implicitly created Lambda log group never expires, which is a standing charge nobody thinks to look for.

Attribution has a documented hole. When another account publishes to your bus through a resource policy, their account receives the CloudTrail data event and yours does not. Owning the bus does not mean seeing who wrote to it.

Cost

Billed $0.0000281, read from Cost Explorer once AWS posted the charges: CloudTrail data events $0.000013 and EventBridge $0.0000151, both on 24 September. The 25th and 26th billed zero with every resource still running — because there is no hourly meter in this build.

It is worth saying why I waited for the bill. The rate card would have let me publish a number a day earlier. This series once printed $1.40 for a week against a bill of $4.93 by doing exactly that. A calculation is not a measurement, and the gap between them is not always small.

The only standing charge is the archive, at $0.023/GB-month. Replay is billed at the same rate as publishing, $1.00 per million.

Troubleshooting and Lessons

The registry wrote the typo down as a real schema

Problem. Order.crated — published once, matched by nothing, consumed by nobody — acquired a full OpenAPI definition beside the real events.
Root cause. Schema discovery documents what producers actually send, and at the wire level there is no difference between a new event type and a bug.
Resolution. None available — discovery is not validation, and nothing rejected any of them. EventBridge accepted every event regardless of the registry's contents.
Lesson. The mistake does not merely escape detection; it gets written down as though it were intended, where the next engineer reads it as a contract.

The dead-letter queue catches a different failure than you think

Problem. A DLQ was attached to the rule target, an event the consumer fails on was published, and the queue stayed empty.
Root cause. The EventBridge DLQ covers "I could not hand this over". A target that accepts an event and then fails has succeeded as far as the rule is concerned.
Resolution. A separate on-failure destination on the function itself — two failure modes, two queues.
Lesson. Check which half of the handover your DLQ actually guards.

Replay re-publishes into the present

Problem. A replayed event was delivered through a rule that did not exist when it was first published.
Root cause. Replay follows current rules, issues new event ids, and carries today's consequences.
Resolution. Treat replay as publishing, not as time travel.
Lesson. There is no way back to the original publisher, and no way to replay "as it was".

Cleanup and Key Takeaways

The sweep checks four things a "delete the bus" pass misses:

  • The archive is not deleted with the bus, and its storage keeps billing.
  • Discovered schemas outlive the discoverer. They live in the AWS-managed registry and cost nothing — which is exactly why they accumulate, including that typo.
  • The log-group resource policy is account-level and invisible in the log-group view.
  • Rules left on the default bus, which is where they land if event_bus_name is ever omitted from a rule or its target.

The replay test also creates a probe rule outside Terraform, so it removes it on exit via a trap. A script that creates AWS resources outside Terraform creates things the teardown cannot see.

Key takeaways

  • A 200 from PutEvents means the bus accepted your event. Nothing else. Not that anything was listening, not that anything was delivered.
  • There is no metric for "matched no rule". The detection has to be built, and the only way to build it costs a second delivery of every event.
  • A schema registry records mistakes as faithfully as intentions. Discovery is not validation.
  • Your dead-letter queue probably catches a different failure than you think. A target that accepts an event and then fails needs its own mechanism.
  • Replay re-publishes into the present — current rules, new event ids, today's consequences.
