AWS Architecture Series · #111 · Reliability

A stopped canary looks exactly like a healthy service

A canary proves what a metric alarm cannot: that a path works when nobody is using it. But its metrics come from its runs, so when it stops running it publishes the same thing a healthy service does — nothing.

Jayanth Katta Nov 2026 AWS Well-Architected · Reliability · Operational Excellence

Business Challenge

#110 established that an alarm with no data goes to INSUFFICIENT_DATA by default, and that INSUFFICIENT_DATA fires nothing. Canaries are where that behaviour does the most damage, because of what a canary is for.

“Canaries follow the same routes and perform the same actions as a customer, which makes it possible for you to continually verify your customer experience even when you don't have any customer traffic on your applications.”

That is the genuine capability: a metric alarm can only tell you about traffic that happened, so it cannot distinguish working from unused. A canary manufactures the traffic. The catch is that the canary is now the thing that has to keep working.

1No runs means no datapoints, and no datapoints means no alarm

Canary metrics are a product of canary runs. “By default, canaries create several CloudWatch metrics in the CloudWatchSynthetics namespace”, and the metrics themselves are defined per run — “Duration – The duration in milliseconds of the canary run”, “SuccessPercent – The percentage of the runs of this canary that succeed and find no failures”.

So a canary that is stopped, paused, throttled, broken on a bad deployment of its own script, or sitting behind an expired IAM role emits no new datapoints. Follow that into #110's rules: the evaluation range fills with missing data, the default treatment is “missing”, and “If all data points in the alarm evaluation range are missing, the alarm transitions to INSUFFICIENT_DATA.” Since “An alarm invokes actions only when the alarm changes state”, the ALARM action never fires.

The result is a dashboard on which a stopped canary and a perfectly healthy service are the same picture. Worse than a false negative, because a false negative at least implies something was measured.

Fix

Set the canary alarm's missing-data treatment to breaching — “Missing data points are treated as “bad” and breaching the threshold”. For a synthetic monitor this is almost always right: the whole point is that data should always be arriving, so its absence is itself the finding. Then add a second alarm on Failed, which catches the different failure in challenge 2.

2Failed is about the canary; SuccessPercent is about the run

Two metrics that read like synonyms and are not:

“Failed – The number of canary runs that failed to execute. These failures are related to the canary itself.”

“SuccessPercent – The percentage of the runs of this canary that succeed and find no failures.”

Failed counts the canary breaking. SuccessPercent counts runs that completed and found nothing wrong. A script with a syntax error after an update, a canary whose Lambda times out, a broken dependency in its own layer — those move Failed, and they are statements about your monitoring, not about your service. Alarming on one and reporting it as the other is how a monitoring outage gets filed as an application incident, or the reverse.

There is a third reading of the same run: “SuccessPercentWithRetries – The percentage of the runs of this canary that succeed after all attempts”, with “RetryCount – The number of times your canary retried. This metric is only displayed when there are retries.” A path that fails and then succeeds on retry scores badly on SuccessPercent and perfectly on SuccessPercentWithRetries. Both numbers are true; they answer different questions, and the second is the more forgiving one to put on a slide.

Fix

Alarm on all three deliberately and label them for what they measure: Failed as monitoring is broken, SuccessPercent as first attempt failed, SuccessPercentWithRetries as the path is unusable. Which one belongs in your SLO is a real decision — a customer who succeeds on a browser retry had a worse experience than the retried number suggests and a better one than the raw number does.

3The dimensions that exist depend on the canary's own configuration

An alarm names a metric and its dimensions. For canaries, which dimensions exist is not fixed:

“For multi-browser canaries, browser-dimension metrics are enabled by default to provide visibility into performance across browsers like Chrome, Firefox and other browsers.” And the inverse: “For single-browser canaries, browser-dimension metrics are disabled by default to avoid redundancy. To see metrics broken down by browser, set browserDimension to true.”

Step-level dimensions are conditional too: “Canaries that use either the executeStep() or executeHttpStep() methods from the Synthetics library also publish SuccessPercent and Duration metrics with the dimensions CanaryName and StepName for each step.”

So an alarm written against a Browser or StepName dimension depends on a property of the canary's script and configuration. Convert a multi-browser canary to single-browser, or refactor a script away from executeStep(), and the dimension stops being published. #110 covers what happens next: no datapoints, INSUFFICIENT_DATA, no action — and nothing in CloudWatch objects to an alarm on a dimension that no longer exists.

Fix

Alarm on the CanaryName dimension for the signal that must never go quiet, and treat Browser and StepName alarms as additional detail rather than the primary guard. If a script refactor is in scope, check the alarms that reference its step names in the same change — they are coupled to the code, and nothing links them.

Architecture

What a canary proves that a metric alarm cannot

Metric alarm on real trafficCanary
Needs customers Yes — no traffic, no signal No — “even when you don't have any customer traffic”
Can distinguish working from unused No Yes
Covers the whole path Only the hops that emit metrics End to end, as the customer sees it
What goes quiet when it breaks Nothing — the service is still emitting The signal itself

The last row is the trade. A metric alarm's signal is produced by the thing being watched, so it keeps arriving while the watcher is fine. A canary's signal is produced by the watcher, so it stops when either one breaks — and the two cases are indistinguishable without a second alarm.

Three readings of one run

MetricWhat it countsWhat it is a statement about
Failed Runs that “failed to execute” Your monitoring — “related to the canary itself”
SuccessPercent Runs that “succeed and find no failures” The first attempt against your service
SuccessPercentWithRetries Runs that “succeed after all attempts” Whether the path is usable at all
Failed requests “HTTP requests executed by the canary on the target website that failed with no response” Individual requests, not runs

Failed requests is the one most likely to be misread, because it counts requests rather than runs and it is scoped to no response — a 500 is not a failed request by this definition, it is a 5xx.

What else a canary can watch

Beyond availability and latency, canaries can compare what the page looks like: “VisualMonitoringSuccessPercent – The percentage of visual comparisons that successfully matched the baseline screenshots during a canary run.” And the broader claim: “Canaries check the availability and latency of your endpoints and can store load time data and screenshots of the UI.”

Visual monitoring is the capability with no metric-alarm equivalent at all. A service can return 200 with correct latency and a blank page, and nothing in a conventional metric notices. It is also the capability most likely to produce alarm fatigue, because a baseline that drifts with every legitimate design change needs maintaining.

The cost shape, which follows from the schedule

“Canaries can run as often as once per minute” — 1,440 runs a day, each one a Lambda invocation, because “They create Lambda functions in your account that use Node.js, Python, or Java as the runtime.” A headless-browser canary is not a cheap Lambda, and the artefacts it stores accumulate in S3.

So the per-minute schedule that makes a canary responsive is also the one that makes it the most expensive monitoring you own per signal. The useful question is not how often can it run but how long are you willing not to know, which for most paths is longer than a minute.

Why This Architecture Holds Up

Manufacturing the traffic is the only way to answer the question

There is no passive way to know whether an unused path works. Either you wait for a customer to find out, or you generate the request yourself. Synthetics takes the second option honestly, and the consequence — that the monitor becomes a component with its own failure modes — is inherent rather than a design flaw.

Separating Failed from SuccessPercent is the right distinction

It would have been easier to publish one success number and let canary failures contaminate it. Keeping “failures … related to the canary itself” in a separate metric is what makes it possible to tell a monitoring outage from an application outage at all. The names could be clearer; the separation is correct.

Running on your own Lambda is a defensible trade

Canaries execute in your account, with your IAM role, in your VPC if you choose. That makes them debuggable and lets them reach private endpoints, at the price of being your Lambda to keep working. A fully managed prober could not reach anything private.

The audit worth running

Check the missing-data treatment on every canary alarm. If it is the default, that alarm cannot tell you the canary stopped. This is the single highest-value check in this post and it is a configuration field.

Then confirm something alarms on Failed. Without it, a canary that breaks on its own script update is invisible, and the service it was watching is now unmonitored with a green dashboard.

Finally, list your canary alarms by dimension. Anything on Browser or StepName is coupled to a configuration or a function name that a routine change can remove. Those are the alarms most likely to be quietly watching a metric that no longer exists.

Closing Thought

This run of posts has circled one idea from several directions: a signal's absence is not the same as a signal of health, and most dashboards render them identically. #105 had an index that answered partially. #108 had a compliance status that was an estimate. #110 had the alarm state that means I cannot tell.

Canaries are the sharpest form of it, because here the absence is produced by the monitor rather than the system. Every other signal in this series degrades when the thing being watched degrades. A canary's signal degrades when either the service or the canary degrades, and the two look the same from outside. That is not an argument against canaries — they answer a question nothing else can — it is an argument for the second alarm.

So, two alarms per canary, always: one on the signal, treating missing data as breaching, and one on Failed. The first tells you the service is unwell. The second tells you that the first one has stopped being able to say so.
